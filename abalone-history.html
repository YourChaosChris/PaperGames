The History of Abalone

A modern classic: one of the few abstract strategy games designed in the late 20th century to become a genuine, lasting commercial and competitive success.

Invented in France, 1987

Abalone was designed by two French game designers, Michel Lalet and Laurent Lévi, and first published in France in 1987. The two are said to have spent roughly two years refining the game's deceptively simple rules - a hexagonal board, plain marbles, and just one way to capture - before settling on the design that was eventually published.

The game's name is a playful nod to the abalone sea snail, whose shell the round playing pieces were meant to evoke, though the marbles used in most commercial sets are plain glass rather than actual shell.

An Award-Winning Debut

Abalone made a strong first impression: in 1988 it won the Grand Prix du Jouet (France's "Toy of the Year" grand prize) in the games category, a rare distinction for a purely abstract strategy game with no theme, no dice, and no luck at all. The award helped the game find a wide commercial audience well beyond the usual abstract-games hobbyist circle.

Over the following decades Abalone was picked up and re-released by a number of different publishers internationally, along with officially licensed electronic versions, and it has been a fixture of the annual Mind Sports Olympiad and other abstract-games tournaments.

A Lasting Place Among Abstract Games

What made Abalone stand out was how much strategic depth its minimal rule set produced: no piece is ever removed except by being pushed entirely off the board by a strictly larger line of opposing marbles, which puts a premium on group cohesion and central control rather than piece-for-piece exchanges. That single, elegant mechanism has kept the game in print for decades and earned it a regular place alongside older classics like Go and checkers in surveys and collections of great abstract strategy games.
