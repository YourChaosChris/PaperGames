The History of Hnefatafl

One of the oldest board game families in Northern Europe, played across the Viking world for centuries before chess ever arrived there.

A Viking-Age Board Game

Hnefatafl ("king's table") belongs to the wider Tafl family of board games, played across Scandinavia, the British Isles, and Northern Europe from at least the Iron Age through the early medieval period - long before chess reached the region from the south.

Archaeological finds of boards and pieces turn up from Ireland to Ukraine, and the game is mentioned in several Old Norse sagas, including one where a game of tafl is played as Norway's king lies dying - a sign of how thoroughly it was woven into everyday life.

An Asymmetric Siege

Unlike chess, the two sides in Tafl don't mirror each other at all: a small defending force protects a king in the center, badly outnumbered by a larger surrounding army - and each side is chasing a completely different goal, escape versus capture, rather than the shared goal of "eliminate the enemy king".

That asymmetry is also why so many variants exist: board sizes from 7x7 up to 19x19 and differing capture rules for the king are attested across different regions and centuries, since - unlike chess - no single rulebook survived to standardize it before the game fell out of common use after chess spread throughout Europe.

PaperGames plays the smaller Brandub variant (7x7, historically associated with Ireland) with a clearly-defined, self-consistent ruleset for movement and capture - since surviving historical sources genuinely disagree on some of the finer edge cases, rather than picking one specific medieval reconstruction to follow to the letter.
