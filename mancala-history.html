The History of Mancala

One of the oldest game families on Earth.

Pits Carved in Stone

Boards for mancala-family games have been found carved directly into stone across Africa and the Middle East, some estimated at well over a thousand years old, though - as with many ancient games - exact dating and origin are debated among historians.

What's clear is that pit-and-seed games spread and diversified enormously over the centuries, producing hundreds of regional variants across Africa, the Middle East and Asia, from Oware and Bao to Congkak - each with its own board size, seed count, and capture rules, all sharing the same core idea of sowing.

One Family, Countless Variants

Unlike chess or Go, which each settled into one dominant global ruleset, mancala games never fully converged: the family is defined more by its shared mechanic - sowing seeds one by one around a board of pits - than by any single fixed set of rules.

That variety is part of why mancala games remain deeply embedded in everyday culture across much of Africa and Asia, often played casually in markets and courtyards with nothing more than a board scratched into the ground and a handful of seeds or pebbles.

Kalaha: A Standardized Ruleset

The specific ruleset PaperGames uses - two rows of six pits, a store at each end, capture into an empty pit, and an extra turn for landing in your own store - is Kalaha, a mancala variant that was trademarked and popularized as a mass-produced board game in the mid-20th century United States.

Kalaha's clean, precisely defined rules made it a natural fit for computers early on, and it's now one of the most common mancala rulesets found in digital games and apps worldwide, including this one.
